Moving out is exhausting enough without the second fight: the deposit. You hand back the keys, the landlord keeps the deposit — or far more of it than seems right — and sends an itemized list that reads like a renovation invoice. Most tenants argue, get nowhere, and give up.

Do not argue. Document, demand in writing, and follow the order. The deposit is your money until the landlord proves a reason to keep part of it — in writing.

## Start before you leave

The fight is mostly won before move-out day. Two habits beat any letter you will write later:

**Take photos.** Photograph every room, wall, appliance, and surface on the way in and on the way out. Date-stamped photos are the closest thing to an unbiased witness you will ever have. If the landlord claims damage you did not cause, your move-out photos answer it in one picture.

**Use checklists.** A move-in checklist and a move-out checklist, filled in room by room, turn "the place was fine" into a record. Note each item's condition, not just a general impression. "Living room carpet: worn thin in front of the sofa" is a record. "Good condition" is an opinion.

Keep a photo log alongside the checklists: what each photo shows, when it was taken, which room. Months later, you will not remember the details. The log will.

## Demand the itemized statement in writing

If any part of your deposit is withheld, you are entitled to an itemized statement of what was kept and why. Do not accept a vague explanation, or a verbal one. Ask in writing, and keep your request in writing too. Every step should leave a paper trail — it is what a court or mediator reads if it ever gets that far.

When the statement arrives, check it line by line against your photos and checklists. Every deduction has to match something real.

## Deadlines are state law — look yours up first

This is the part that trips up the most tenants. Deposit deadlines are state law, and they are different in every state. The return deadline, the deadline for a written demand, the penalty for bad-faith withholding, and the court where you would file — all of it depends on where you rent. Two tenants with the same deposit in different states can have completely different deadlines and penalties.

So the first step, before any letter, is a short state lookup: your state's statute, return deadline, penalty rule, and court limit. It takes about ten minutes, and everything you write after that should quote your state's law — never another state's. A letter citing the wrong state is worse than no letter at all.

## Normal wear is not damage

This is the line the whole fight usually turns on, so learn it cold:

**Normal wear** is what happens when a place is lived in normally: faded paint, small nail holes, carpet worn thin in walkways, dusty blinds. That is the landlord's cost of doing business, not a deduction from your deposit.

**Damage** is different: large holes in walls, burns, pet damage, trash left behind, broken fixtures. That is the kind of thing a landlord can fairly deduct for.

The audit is simple once you see the line: sort every deduction in the landlord's statement into one of the two columns. Accept what is fair. Fight what is not. Tenants lose credibility disputing everything; they win it conceding the fair deductions and fighting the rest with photos.

## Letters, in the right order

When the statement is wrong, write — in this order:

1. **Written demand.** A clear letter asking for the return of the deposit, or the wrongly withheld part of it, with a deadline.
2. **Penalty letter.** If the landlord withheld in bad faith or blew the deadline, a letter saying so, citing your state's penalty rule.
3. **Small-claims warning.** A final letter saying you will file in small claims court if the money is not returned. Many landlords settle here, because court costs them time too.

If one deduction is wrong and the rest are fair, dispute just that one. A one-deduction dispute letter is short, specific, and hard to argue with.
