A medical bill arrives, and most people do the same thing: stare at the total, feel sick twice, and pay it. It looks official — your name, a big number, a due date. It feels like a verdict.

It is not a verdict. It is a claim. And claims can be wrong.

## Why medical bills carry errors

A medical bill passes through a lot of hands before it reaches you: the provider, the coder, the billing office, and your insurance company. At every step, something can slip. The errors that show up most often are ordinary, not exotic:

- **Duplicate charges** — the same service billed twice.
- **Wrong quantities** — billed for two of something when you received one.
- **Services you never received** — a line item for care that never happened.
- **Insurance payments missing from your balance** — your insurer paid, but the payment never made it onto your bill, so you are being asked to cover it again.

Nobody checks it for you. You have to check it yourself, because the bill will not check itself.

## Step one: get the itemized bill

The summary bill most people receive is not enough to audit anything. It shows a total and maybe a few broad categories. You need the itemized bill — the line-by-line version that lists every service, every charge, and every payment applied.

Call the billing office and ask for it by name: "Please send me an itemized bill." Do it before you pay anything — getting money back is a much harder fight than getting a charge corrected first.

## Step two: walk it line by line

With the itemized bill in hand, go through it the way an auditor would: every line has to earn its place. Ask of each charge:

- Did I actually receive this service?
- Is the quantity right?
- Does the charge match what my insurance already paid?
- Is there a payment or adjustment missing from my balance?

Mark anything that fails. Then ask for a coding review — a polite request for the billing office to re-check how a charge was coded. Coding mistakes are common, and a review is routine to ask for.

## Know your rights before you pay

A few rights are worth knowing by name, because they change what you can ask for:

- **If you are uninsured, you have the right to a Good Faith Estimate** before non-emergency care. And if the final bill lands $400 or more over that estimate, you can dispute it through a federal process.
- **Emergency care from an out-of-network doctor is generally protected by the No Surprises Act.** You should not be balance-billed the way patients used to be in that situation.
- **If insurance denies a claim, the appeal deadline is printed on your Explanation of Benefits (EOB).** That deadline is real, so read the EOB even when the news in it is bad.

Write these down with your bill. A cheat sheet of your rights turns a scary phone call into a checklist.

## The discount most people never ask for

Nonprofit hospitals are required to offer financial assistance, usually called charity care. Here is the part most people miss: you get at least 240 days from your first bill to apply, even if you think you earn too much to qualify. Most people never apply because nobody tells them the program exists.

Apply before you negotiate. The order matters — a discount granted through financial assistance changes the number you are negotiating about, and you cannot negotiate a number you have not yet reduced.

You need the hospital's financial assistance policy, which it has to make available, plus whatever documents it asks for. Track every application: the date, who you spoke to, and what was decided.

## Put it in writing

Phone calls have their place — the itemized bill request, the coding review — but the fight itself belongs in writing. A written dispute, a written appeal, a written hardship request, and a written request for debt validation all do something a phone call cannot: they create a record with a date on it.

Be polite, be specific, and be persistent — polite, written, and persistent beats angry every time. Keep a copy of everything you send and receive.
