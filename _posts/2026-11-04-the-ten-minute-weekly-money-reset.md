Most money advice asks for a big overhaul: a new budget, a new system, a new life. That is why most of it never happens. What actually works is smaller and duller: ten minutes, once a week, same day, same four steps.

Pick a day and a time and keep it. Sunday evening works for a lot of people, because the week ahead is visible from there. Any day works, though, as long as it is the same day every week. Put a recurring reminder on your phone. When it goes off, set a timer for ten minutes and do these four things.

## 1. Glance at your balances (2 minutes)

Open your bank app and look at two numbers: checking and savings. That is it. You are not analyzing anything. You are just noticing. Is checking lower than you expected? Is savings where it should be? Most weeks, nothing will surprise you, and that is the point — you are building the habit of looking, so the week something is off, you catch it on day one instead of day twenty.

## 2. Scan last week's transactions (3 minutes)

Scroll through the last seven days of spending. You are looking for three things only: something you do not recognize, something charged twice, and something you forgot about. Subscriptions are the usual culprits in that third category — the free trial that converted, the service you stopped using months ago.

If you find an error, do not fix it now. Write it down — a note on your phone, a line on paper — and handle it the next day. The reset is for noticing, not for phone calls. Keeping it to ten minutes is what keeps you doing it next week.

## 3. Move the money (2 minutes)

Two moves, if they apply this week: the savings transfer, if it is not automatic, and anything due before your next reset. A bill due Wednesday gets scheduled now, not remembered on Wednesday. If money needs to move between accounts for a bill, move it now while you are looking.

This step is the one that prevents the two most common money headaches: overdrafts and missed due dates. Both usually happen not because the money did not exist, but because nobody looked at the right moment. This is the right moment, once a week, on purpose.

## 4. Look at the week ahead (3 minutes)

Ask one question: what costs money this week that I should know about now? A birthday, a school fee, a car appointment, a week with three takeout nights because nobody has time to cook. You are not solving the week. You are just removing surprises from it.

If the week looks tight, that is useful information, not a verdict. Knowing on Sunday that Thursday is tight beats discovering it on Thursday. A tight week you saw coming is a week you can plan around — cook twice, skip one outing, move one purchase to next week.

## Why weekly beats monthly

A monthly money day sounds more efficient, and it fails for the same reason a monthly gym visit fails: the gap between sessions is where everything falls apart. In thirty days, a wrong charge becomes a forgotten charge. A subscription renews twice. A balance drifts for weeks before anyone notices.

Weekly is short enough that nothing gets far before you see it, and long enough that it never feels like a chore. Ten minutes is also short enough that you will actually do it when you are tired, busy, or not in the mood — which is most weeks, honestly.

## The rules that keep it working

Keep it to ten minutes. If it runs long, you are doing extra work that belongs somewhere else. The timer is not a gimmick; it is the boundary that protects the habit.

Do not use the ten minutes to beat yourself up. If the week was expensive, notice it and move on. The reset is a windshield, not a rearview mirror. Its job is the road ahead.

Skip a week without quitting. Life happens. A missed reset is not a broken system — it is one missed reset. Do it next week, same day, same four steps. The people who stick with this are not the ones who never miss. They are the ones who miss and come back.

That is the whole system. Ten minutes, four steps, once a week. Boring beats brilliant, every time, because boring is what you will still be doing in six months.
