You lost the job. Now there are deadlines, bills, and a job search — all at once, while you are still processing the shock. Most people do the worst possible thing in the first three days: nothing, because they do not know what to do first — or everything at once, because panic picks the order.

There is an order. Follow it, and the first 72 hours stop being chaos and become a plan.

## First: HR questions, in writing

Before anything else, get answers from your former employer — in writing. Email beats a phone call, because you want a record. Ask:

- What is my final pay, and when will it arrive?
- Is there severance, and what are its terms?
- When does my health coverage end?
- Who do I contact about benefits questions after this week?

Do not sign anything in the first meeting if you can avoid it. Take severance paperwork home, read it properly, and ask questions in writing before you sign. A signature given in shock is hard to undo.

## Second: file for unemployment

File as soon as you can. Unemployment insurance is state-run: the agency, filing process, deadlines, and weekly certification rules all depend on your state. There is no single national number or form.

The generic steps are the same everywhere: find your state's agency, file your initial claim, then certify each week as your state requires. The details — which agency, site, and certification day — go on a lookup worksheet from your own state's agency. Do not trust a national article for your state's deadline. Look yours up.

A weekly certification tracker helps: one row per week, what you certified, what happened. When a week goes wrong — a missed certification, a payment delay — the tracker tells you exactly what to ask about instead of guessing.

## Third: health coverage, before the clocks run out

This is the step people miss, because the deadlines are real and they run at the same time. Two federal windows matter:

- **COBRA:** if your employer offers it, you generally have at least 60 days to elect COBRA continuation coverage. That window is longer than it feels like in week one, but not open forever.
- **Marketplace:** losing job-based coverage qualifies you for a special enrollment period, generally 60 days, on the Marketplace.

Compare both before either window closes. Picking one does not extend the other, and the right choice depends on your doctors, prescriptions, and budget — not on which letter scared you more. Medicaid and CHIP are also worth checking; applications are accepted any time of year.

Write down the dates from your own notices — they beat anything printed anywhere else.

## Fourth: money triage

Now the bills. Do not pay them in the order they arrive. Pay them in the order of consequence.

Rank every bill by what happens if you do not pay it: eviction and utility shutoffs at the top, unsecured debts lower. Then work out your cash runway — how many months your savings cover at current spending. That number tells you whether to cut, call, or find bridge income, and how urgently.

Then call before a payment is late, not after. A short hardship script beats a long apology: what happened, what you are asking for, when you will pay. Creditors hear "I lost my job" every day. What they remember is a caller with a plan and a date.

## Fifth: the search, on a rhythm

Only now does the job search start — with a tracker, not panic-applying. Log every application: company, role, date, contact, next step. A search without a tracker feels busy and goes nowhere; with one, you see what is actually moving.

Set a weekly rhythm instead of an all-day grind: set applications, follow-up, and a stop time. The search is a marathon you did not sign up for, and pace beats panic.

Refresh the resume while you are at it: a short refresh that says what you do, not your whole life story. Line up references early, before a hiring manager asks — a request sent now is easy, one sent in a rush is not.
